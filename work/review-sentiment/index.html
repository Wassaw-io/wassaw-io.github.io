Reviews did not predict sales, and they caught a bad batch a month early

A store wanted to know whether the sentiment of its product reviews could forecast demand. Across products, better reviews went with higher sales. Within a product, a change in sentiment said nothing about next month’s sales. What reviews did well was raise an alarm when one product had a problem.

ProblemWhether review sentiment for 40 products could forecast demand, and if not, what it is useful for.

What I didScored 11,751 written reviews with a sentiment model, compared the relationship with sales across products and within each product over time, and built an alarm for months when a product’s sentiment falls far below its own history.

DecisionDo not use sentiment to forecast sales. Use it as a product-problem alarm: at the chosen setting it flagged the bad batch in the month it reached customers, a month before sales fell 45%, with 4 false alarms in 714 product-months.

A standard report against this analysis

A standard report

Plots average sentiment against sales for each product, finds a strong correlation (0.61 here), and proposes a sentiment dashboard for forecasting demand.

This analysis

Compares each product with itself over time, where the correlation is 0.00, so sentiment would not have forecast anything. It then uses the same scores as an alarm for product problems, which caught a bad batch a month before sales fell.

When the simple method is enough: if the question is which products customers like, the comparison across products answers it. The trouble starts only when that comparison is used to predict what happens next.

Across products, and within a product

Better products sell more and collect better reviews, so across the 40 products, sentiment and sales move together. That says reviews describe which products are popular. A forecast needs something different: that when a product’s reviews improve, its sales rise afterward. Comparing each product with itself, month to month, tests that directly.

What reviews are good for

Partway through the period, one of the best-selling products had a bad batch. Its reviews turned negative in the month the batch reached customers, and its sales fell about 45% the month after. The alarm compares each month’s sentiment with that product’s own history and counts only months with at least five reviews. The setting decides how many false alarms the team accepts.

What holds generally, and what is specific to this data

The main lesson holds generally: a correlation across units (products, stores, customers) does not show that changing one thing within a unit changes the other, and a forecast needs the second. Comparing each unit with itself over time is the usual check. Reviews also tend to be more useful for spotting problems than for predicting demand, since a problem shows up in the text before it shows up in returns and sales.

The sizes are specific to this data: the 0.61 and 0.00 correlations, the one-month lead, and the false-alarm counts.

Where else this applies

The same comparison applies to social media mentions and stock or ticket sales, survey scores and customer retention, and employee engagement and turnover. The alarm applies to support tickets, app store reviews, and patient feedback.

Details for technical readers

Scoring. VADER compound score per review, averaged per product-month. Months with no reviews are left missing rather than filled.

Within-product test. First differences of monthly sentiment against the next month’s first difference of log units, which removes each product’s fixed level. The product with the bad batch is left out so one event does not drive the estimate. Slope 0.001, 95% interval −0.074 to 0.072 from a bootstrap that resamples whole products, since months of the same product are not independent.

Alarm. For each product-month, z = (sentiment − mean of earlier months) / standard deviation of earlier months, with at least six earlier months and five reviews in the current month. An alarm fires when z falls at or below the setting.
