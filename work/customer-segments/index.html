Customer segments that describe behavior, and pairings that are not just milk

A grocery and household store wanted to know how its customers differ and which products to place, bundle, or promote together. The obvious version of each analysis gives an answer that looks reasonable and says very little. The fix for both was small.

ProblemHow the store’s 4,000 customers differ, and which products to place, bundle, or promote together.

What I didBuilt customer features and product-pair rules in SQL, compared k-means with and without scaling, picked the number of segments by silhouette score, and ranked pair rules by lift instead of confidence.

DecisionFour segments, each with its own action, led by weekly regulars (44% of spend) and a lapsing group to win back. Bundle and place the eight pairs bought together about six times as often as chance, and ignore the high-confidence rules that only point at milk.

When the simple method is enough: if the only question is who spends the most, spend bands answer it and need no model. Segmentation earns its keep when each segment gets a different action.

Segments

Each customer is described by five numbers: days since the last order, number of orders, average order value, total spend, and the share of spend on discount. Total spend is in thousands of dollars and discount share is a fraction, so without scaling, k-means measures distance almost entirely in dollars and the segments come out as spending bands. After standardizing, the silhouette score was highest at four segments, and those four match how the customers actually shop.

Scaling turns spending bands into shopping behaviors

Each dot is a customer, placed by the two main directions of variation in the standardized features. Colors are the four k-means segments from each version.

The actions differ by segment. Weekly regulars carry the largest share of spend and already come back, so discounts on what they buy anyway mostly give away margin. Monthly stock-up customers buy large baskets, which is where bundles and placement of paired items pay. Deal seekers respond to promotions, so each promotion should be tested against a holdout before it is scaled. The lapsing group is small in spend (4%) but is the one where a win-back offer has something to recover.

Four segments fit best, and only after scaling

Pairings

A pair rule says that customers who buy one product also buy another. Confidence is the usual way to rank them, and it rewards any rule that ends in a product most baskets contain anyway. Here milk is in 72% of baskets, so every rule ending in milk has confidence near 0.72 and says nothing. Lift corrects for that.

Ranked by confidence, every top rule ends in milk

The top ten pair rules, computed in SQL over 96,000 baskets. Switch the ranking, or raise the minimum lift.

A rule shows that two products are bought together. It does not show that a bundle or a discount will change what anyone buys, since customers buying burger buns were going to buy ground beef anyway. Measuring that needs a holdout, which is what the who-to-call case study does for retention calls.

What holds generally, and what is specific to this data

Two lessons hold for any store. Distance-based clustering on features in different units is driven by whichever feature has the largest numbers, so features need to be put on a common scale first. And confidence favors rules that end in popular products, so pair rules should be ranked by lift (or a similar measure that accounts for how common each product is).

The sizes are specific to this data: the four segments and their shares, the agreement scores of 0.83 and 0.16, and the lift of about six for the real pairs.

Where else this applies

The same segmentation applies to members of a gym, subscribers to a service, patients of a clinic, or accounts of a software company. The pair rules apply to any catalog: online stores, restaurant menus, add-on services, and content people read or watch together.

Details for technical readers

Features. Built in SQL (customer_features.sql), then standardized to mean 0 and variance 1. k-means with 10 starts for each k from 2 to 8. The silhouette score uses a sample of 2,000 customers. Agreement with the generating behaviors is the adjusted Rand index, which is 0 for random labels and 1 for a perfect match. The 2D view is the first two principal components of the standardized features.

Rules. One self-join of the order lines on order ID (pair_rules.sql), with

support = n(A, B) / N,   confidence = n(A, B) / n(A),   lift = confidence / (n(B) / N)

Pairs seen in fewer than 20 baskets are dropped. The tests check support, confidence, and lift against a direct count of baskets, and check that lift is symmetric while confidence is not. This is the two-item case of Apriori, which is all a placement or bundling decision usually needs.
