Staff to the top of the forecast, not the middle

A tutoring center needed to know how many instructor hours to schedule for the next nine months. A forecast gives a range, and the right place in that range depends on what each kind of mistake costs. Here that put the schedule well above the middle, and it cut the expected cost of staffing mistakes by about a quarter.

ProblemHow many instructor hours to schedule each month, when enrollment swings with the school year.

What I didChecked the center’s before-and-after numbers against the school calendar, compared four forecasting methods on 22 past starting points, and turned the best forecast into a schedule using the cost of an idle hour against the cost of an hour short.

DecisionSchedule to about the 77th percentile of each month’s forecast instead of the middle. Over nine months that cut the expected cost of staffing mistakes from about $5,140 to about $3,880, using conservative costs.

A standard report against this analysis

A standard report

Forecasts each month as the same month last year, or from a trend line, and schedules staff to that single number.

It does not say how far off the number could be, so it has no way to decide how much buffer to keep.

This analysis

Tests four forecasting methods on past data and keeps the one that wins (SARIMA, 16% more accurate than “same month last year”).

It gives each month a range, then schedules to the point in that range where the cost of idle hours and the cost of short hours balance. Over nine months that cuts the expected cost of staffing mistakes by about $1,260, or 25%.

When the simple method is enough: if an idle hour and a short hour cost about the same, the middle of the forecast is the right schedule, and a careful “same month last year” gets most of the way there. The gain here comes from the two costs being different, which they usually are when being short means turning customers away.

The schedule

Staffing follows the active roster, so the forecast is of active students, converted to instructor hours at 5.2 sessions per student each month and three students per instructor hour. An idle hour costs the instructor’s wage, $24. An hour short costs at least the margin on three student sessions, about $82 ($315 monthly tuition over 5.2 sessions, at a 45% margin). That is a conservative figure, since a family turned away in September may not come back at all.

With those two costs, the best schedule sits at the 77th percentile of each month’s forecast. If a short hour costs about $216 or more (2.6 times one session’s margin), the best schedule reaches the top of the 80% range.

How the forecast was checked

Before forecasting anything, I checked the improvement the center’s other reports relied on. Leads swing by about 63 a month between the busiest and slowest months of the school year, which is larger than most operating changes, so a before-and-after comparison can partly measure the calendar. After removing the seasonal pattern with an STL decomposition, the lift in leads went from +31.8% to +34.5%.

Lead lift, before against after
Raw comparison+31.8%
With the seasonal pattern removed+34.5%

The calendar was working against the result, so the raw comparison understated the improvement rather than inflating it. The correction is small here because both windows cover more than a full year. On a six-month comparison it could be much larger.

Then I compared four methods on 22 past starting points, each forecasting three months ahead with only the data available at that point (a backtest). “Same month last year” takes one line of code and beat the Fourier regression. SARIMA beat it by 16%, which is a real gain and also a modest one, about what to expect from 54 months of monthly data.

What holds generally, and what is specific to this data

The rule for where to schedule holds for any business: the best level is the percentile equal to the cost of being short divided by the sum of both costs. It is a standard result (the newsvendor rule), not a feature of this data, and it means scheduling to the middle of a forecast is only right when the two costs are equal. The habit of comparing every forecast with a simple baseline carries over as well.

The sizes are specific to this center. The 16% gain over “same month last year”, the 77th percentile, and the $1,260 saving all depend on its costs and its history.

Where else this applies

The same forecast and rule apply to nurse and front-desk shifts at clinics, staff and perishable stock at restaurants and shops, agents per hour on support teams, and seasonal hiring ahead of a busy period. What these have in common is a commitment made before demand is known, where too much and too little cost different amounts.

Details for technical readers

Forecast. SARIMA(1,0,1)(1,1,0)[12] fitted to 54 months of active students, with an 80% interval. Hours are students × 5.2 ÷ 3.

Backtest. Rolling origin, 22 origins, three-month horizon, at least 30 months of training data, scored on monthly new enrollments. MAE and RMSE are in the table under the chart above.

Seasonality check. STL with a 12-month period and a robust fit, so that one unusual enrollment season does not bend the seasonal component around itself.

Decision rule. Let D be the hours needed in a month, s the hours scheduled, co the cost of an idle hour, and cs the cost of an hour short. The expected cost is

C(s) = co E[(s − D)+] + cs E[(D − s)+]

Setting its derivative to zero gives coF(s) − cs(1 − F(s)) = 0, so the best schedule satisfies

F(s*) = cs / (cs + co)

With D approximately normal with mean μ and standard deviation σ (the 80% half-width divided by 1.2816), and z = (s − μ)/σ, the expected hours short and idle are

E[(D − s)+] = σ[φ(z) − z(1 − Φ(z))]    E[(s − D)+] = σ[φ(z) + zΦ(z)]

Caveats. Each month is treated on its own, with no carry-over of staff between months. SARIMA’s intervals assume the model is correct, and intervals from a model fitted to 54 months are often too narrow in practice. A wider range moves the best schedule further above the middle, not below it.
