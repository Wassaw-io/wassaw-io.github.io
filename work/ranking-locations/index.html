Ranking locations by churn rate ranks the noise

A franchise owner wanted to know which of fourteen centers was doing worst, so the regional director could visit and fix it. The usual ranking by churn rate treats a 45-student center and a 222-student center as equally reliable. After accounting for the difference, none of the fourteen could be called worse than average.

ProblemWhich of fourteen centers has a real retention problem, when they range from 45 to 222 students.

What I didFit a hierarchical model that pulls each center’s rate toward the group average in proportion to how little data it has, checked whether each ranking held up from one quarter to the next, and tested where the method fails.

DecisionStop publishing the raw ranking, and report each center with its range. No center was worse than average at 90% confidence, so a $4,200 visit to the bottom center was not supported by the data.

This analysis

Gives each center a pooled rate and a 90% range. The pooled ranking held up better from one quarter to the next (rank correlation 0.72 against 0.53).

Every center’s range included the group average, so the data cannot say which center is worst.

When the simple method is enough: when every location has a few hundred customers or more, raw rates are already fairly stable and pooling changes little. It matters most when some locations are small, which is common for franchises, clinics, and sales territories.

Pulling each center toward the average

A center with 45 students can move several places in the ranking with three extra departures. So each center’s pooled rate is a weighted average of its own rate and the group average, with more weight on its own rate the more students it has. The data sets the weights: here, a 45-student center is pulled 85% of the way toward the average and a 222-student center 54% of the way.

Does the ranking hold up?

This is the check I would put in front of an owner, since it needs no statistics to follow. Rank the centers on one quarter, then compare with the next quarter. A real ordering repeats, and noise does not.

The highest chance that any one center is worse than average came out at 79% (center M), short of the 90% that would justify acting on it.

Where the method fails

On this particular sample, pooling made the point estimates worse, not better (mean squared error 0.000359 against 0.000206 for the raw rates). The reason is that the strength of pooling is fitted from the same fourteen centers, and with that few groups the fit tends to pool too hard. Here it came out at 262 against a true value of 50.

So I measured how this behaves as the number of locations grows, over 25 simulated data sets at each size. Pooling reduced error on average at every size, and the fitted strength settled near the true value from about fifty locations. It can still lose on a single data set, since the guarantee is about the average over many samples. With few locations the better model also treats the pooling strength as uncertain, instead of fixing it at one fitted value.

What holds generally, and what is specific to this data

The main lesson holds for any ranking of units with different sizes: small units swing further by chance, so they are more likely to land at either end of a raw ranking, and pooling with a range is the standard fix. The quarter-to-quarter check carries over as well, and it is a quick way to show an owner whether a ranking means anything. Also general is the caution that a pooling strength fitted from few groups tends to be too strong.

The sizes are specific to this data: the 0.53 and 0.72 correlations, the 79% top probability, and the fitted strength of 262.

Where else this applies

The same method applies to ranking stores, clinics, sales representatives, teachers, ad campaigns, and product categories, and to experiments split into many small segments. It is most useful when someone is about to act on the top or bottom of a table.

Details for technical readers

Model. Leavers yj out of nj students at center j, with

yj ~ Binomial(nj, pj),   pj ~ Beta(a, b)

The posterior is Beta(a + yj, b + nj − yj), with mean

E[pj | yj] = (a + yj) / (a + b + nj)

which is the raw rate and the population mean weighted by nj and κ = a + b, so κ acts as a prior sample size. The shrinkage toward the mean is κ / (κ + nj). (a, b) are fitted by maximizing the Beta-Binomial marginal likelihood: a = 25.2, b = 236.3, κ = 262, mean 9.65%.

Why it over-shrinks. Empirical Bayes plugs in the fitted (a, b) as if known. With fourteen groups the marginal likelihood favors large κ, so estimates are pulled past the truth on some draws. A full hierarchical model with a prior on (a, b) integrates that uncertainty out, which is worth doing at fourteen groups and makes little difference at two hundred.
